# Case Study Page Writer

## 1. Purpose

Case studies are the most persuasive asset in the UniqBrio funnel because they are **evidence, not adjectives**. A prospect reading generic marketing copy has to trust UniqBrio's word. A prospect reading a detailed case study can verify the claim against a real academy's specific numbers, timeline, and voice.

**Why case studies matter:**
- They sit at the bottom of the funnel (BOFU) — the moment a prospect is comparing options and looking for a reason to stop evaluating and act.
- They outperform generic testimonials because they provide *context* (who, what situation, what constraints) plus *chronology* (what happened, in what order) plus *quantification* (what changed, by how much) — a testimonial gives none of this.
- They build trust by demonstrating that UniqBrio understands the specific operational reality of arts and sports academies, not just software in the abstract.
- They reduce perceived risk by letting a prospect mentally rehearse their own adoption journey through someone else's proven one ("if an academy like mine did this, I can too").
- They arm sales conversations with a reusable, link-shareable proof point that pre-empts objections before a call even happens.

## 2. Scope

### In scope — this skill generates:
- Full-length website case study pages (the primary deliverable, typically 1,200–2,500 words)
- Landing-page case study variants (shorter, higher CTA density)
- Downloadable PDF-ready case study content
- Sales one-pager adaptations
- Executive summaries for internal or investor use
- Supporting production artifacts: interview question sets, fact sheets, approval checklists

### Out of scope — this skill does NOT generate:
- Short social testimonials, pull-quotes, or star-rating snippets → use `testimonial-content-builder`
- Tweet threads or other short-form social copy
- Customer quote graphics or image-based proof assets
- Product screenshots, dashboard mockups, or UI renders → use `product-screenshot-mockup-specialist`
- Pure before/after comparison visuals with no surrounding narrative → use `before-after-content-builder`
- Stand-alone technical SEO audits of an already-published page → use `on-page-seo-copywriter`

If a request is for one of the above, hand off to the correct skill rather than producing a shortened case study.

## 3. Product Context (apply throughout)

- **Product:** UniqBrio — India-first B2B SaaS for academy management.
- **Stack:** React Native Expo PWA, Next.js, Supabase, PostgreSQL, Edge Functions, Vercel.
- **Customers:** arts academies, sports academies, music schools, dance schools, martial arts academies, coaching centres, training institutes — mostly small and medium owner-operators in Tier 2/3 Indian cities.
- **Website context:** entirely pre-login marketing content. Case studies are BOFU assets whose job is to remove the last friction before a demo booking, free trial signup, or paid subscription.
- **Core capability areas to reference where relevant:** attendance tracking, fee/payment collection, WhatsApp automation and notifications, student/parent communication, staff and branch management, scheduling, reporting/analytics, multi-branch rollout.

## 4. Case Study Framework

Use this structure as the default skeleton. Every section is adaptable to available material, but do not skip a section silently — if information is missing, say so transparently (see §6 and §13) rather than omitting the section.

1. **Hero** — outcome-led headline, subheadline, 2–4 key metrics, customer logo/industry badge, primary CTA.
2. **Customer Snapshot** — industry, location, academy size (students/staff/branches), products used, partnership duration. If any field is undisclosed, state "Not publicly disclosed" rather than inventing it.
3. **Executive Summary** — 150–200 words covering initial situation, core challenge, why UniqBrio was chosen, and the headline quantified result.
4. **The Challenge** — business context, specific pain points, operational constraints, growth limitations, decision criteria, and the evaluation process/alternatives considered.
5. **The Solution** — why UniqBrio was selected, implementation timeline, onboarding, configuration, workflow changes, automation, integrations (e.g. WhatsApp), staff training, and rollout (including multi-branch sequencing if relevant).
6. **The Results** — organized as Before → After, then broken out by:
- Business impact (revenue, growth)
- Operational impact (time, workflow)
- Financial impact (collection, cost)
- Customer/parent experience
- Student experience
- Staff experience
7. **Business Impact / Broader Outcomes** — visibility, decision-making speed, sustainable growth, retention.
8. **Lessons Learned** — implementation insights, unexpected discoveries, honest reflections.
9. **Future Plans** — expansion, additional modules, growth trajectory.
10. **Customer Quote** — only if a real, approved quote exists (see §8). Never fabricate.
11. **Call to Action** — primary conversion action (demo/trial), placed here and reinforced earlier in the page.
12. **Related Resources / Next Steps** — related case studies, relevant feature pages, FAQ.

## 5. Narrative Architecture — Challenge → Solution → Result

Every case study must read as a coherent story arc, not a features list:

Initial State → Pain/Consequences → Search & Evaluation → Decision
→ Implementation → Adoption → Early Wins → Measured Outcomes
→ Business Transformation → Future Vision

**Story-flow rules:**
- Open with relatable, specific context — not "they needed better management" but "attendance was tracked manually across four coaches, resulting in inconsistent records and delayed parent updates."
- Build credibility gradually: show the customer as a competent operator making a smart evaluation, not a helpless user rescued by software.
- Maintain strict chronological progression — a reader should always know what happened next.
- Explain *why* decisions were made (evaluation criteria, doubts, what tipped the scale) — this is what makes the "Solution" section persuasive rather than promotional.
- Balance hard metrics with human stakes: staff relief, parent trust, student experience. Numbers convince the brain; human detail convinces the gut.
- Never jump directly from Challenge to Results — the reader must understand the causal path connecting them, or the metrics feel unearned.
- Write customer-centric, not product-centric: "The academy reduced admin time" — not "UniqBrio's powerful automation slashed admin time."

## 6. Quantified Metrics Requirement

Every case study **must** contain measurable outcomes wherever it is possible to source them. This is the single most important discipline in this skill.

**Priority metrics to seek, by category:**

| Category | Metrics |
|---|---|
| Financial | Revenue growth, fee collection rate, MRR impact, cost reduction, collection time, customer lifetime value |
| Growth | Enrollment growth, lead-to-enrollment conversion, renewals, expansion, retention |
| Operational | Admin hours saved, manual work reduced, time-to-onboard, automation percentage, support tickets reduced |
| Engagement/Experience | Attendance accuracy, payment completion rate, WhatsApp response rate, parent satisfaction, staff productivity, student/parent/staff experience notes |

**When precise numbers are unavailable:** use transparent, honest qualifying language instead of silence or invention:
- "approximately," "reported," "estimated," "internal measurement," "customer-reported," "according to the academy's internal records"

**Hard rules — never violate these:**
- Never fabricate a statistic, percentage, or ROI figure.
- Never invent a number "for flow" even if it feels plausible.
- Never state a precise figure without a plausible evidentiary basis (see §7).
- If no numbers exist at all, pivot the case study toward qualitative transformation (see §18, "No Metrics Available") rather than manufacturing quantification.

**Acceptable vs. unacceptable example:**
- Acceptable: "Staff reported spending significantly less time preparing attendance summaries after switching from paper registers."
- Acceptable: "The academy estimated a roughly 30% improvement in on-time fee collection within the first quarter."
- Unacceptable: "Saved 83% of administrative time." (stated as fact with no source)

## 7. Evidence Standards

Classify and attribute every factual claim by its evidence tier. Higher tiers should be preferred and cited more confidently; lower tiers require hedging language.

**Evidence hierarchy (highest to lowest confidence):**
1. Verified platform/business records (UniqBrio analytics, customer's own financial systems, billing data)
2. Customer-reported metrics from interviews or written statements (clearly attributed: "According to the academy owner…")
3. Internal analytics / usage data (adoption, feature usage)
4. CRM data, support logs
5. Survey data (state methodology if known)
6. Manual calculations or estimates (state the assumption behind them)
7. Screenshots — only ever reference screenshots that actually exist; never describe a visual that hasn't been provided.

Attribute claims naturally in prose ("Internal usage data shows…", "The owner estimated…") rather than footnoting every sentence. Never invent an attribution source.

## 8. Quote Sourcing Workflow

Interview collection → Transcription → Fact-check → Light grammar cleanup
→ Preserve authentic voice → Customer approval → Legal review (if enterprise/sensitive)
→ Publication

- **Collection:** use a structured interview (see the Interview Notes template, §15) to capture chronological, specific, and emotionally honest material.
- **Editing:** light grammar cleanup is acceptable; altering meaning, tone, or intent is not.
- **Preserve voice:** keep the customer's own phrasing and cadence — do not "corporate-ize" it.
- **Approval:** every direct quote must be sent back to the customer for explicit sign-off before publication.
- **Anonymous customers:** if identity must be withheld, use a descriptive stand-in ("A leading multi-branch dance academy in Tamil Nadu") — never invent a name, title, or academy.
- **Sensitive information:** redact specific revenue figures, student/parent identities, or confidential terms unless explicitly cleared.
- **Legal review:** required for enterprise customers or when disclosing financial figures.
- **Paraphrase vs. direct quote:** paraphrase operational detail or unclear phrasing (with attribution); reserve direct quotes for the emotionally resonant or definitive lines that carry the most persuasive weight.
- **Absolute rules:** never fabricate a quote, never invent a name or title, never attribute a quote without underlying evidence that the customer actually said it.

## 9. Writing Style

**Write with:** professional, credible, specific, evidence-based, conversational, clear, customer-centric, scannable, persuasive, executive-friendly, SEO-friendly, accessible language.

**Avoid entirely:** marketing hype, buzzwords ("revolutionary," "game-changing," "world-class," "seamless," "disruptive"), fluff, empty superlatives ("amazing," "incredible"), unsupported claims, vague benefits ("improved efficiency" without a number), and generic praise ("great product").

**Concrete contrast:**
- Vague: "They needed better management." → Specific: "Attendance was tracked manually across four coaches, resulting in inconsistent records and delayed parent updates."
- Vague: "Improved efficiency." → Specific: "Saved roughly 5 hours per week on attendance reconciliation."

Target reading level: approximately Grade 8–10. Short paragraphs, bolded metrics, and scannable subheads throughout.

## 11. Visual Content Guidance

Written content should be planned to coordinate with (but this skill does not generate) the following visual types: annotated product screenshots, dashboard views, before/after comparison graphics, timeline/rollout graphics, customer logos, growth charts, workflow diagrams, and feature-highlight icon cards.

**Notes for designers:** insert clearly labeled placeholders at the point of need, e.g. `[Design note: dashboard screenshot showing attendance module]`. Never describe a visual in the copy as if it exists when it has not been supplied — use a placeholder instead. Every visual should reinforce the surrounding text, not carry information the text doesn't also state (for accessibility, see §14).

## 12. SEO Guidance

- **SEO Title (<60 chars):** `[Customer Name] Case Study | [Key Result] | UniqBrio`
- **Meta Description (150–160 chars):** summarize the transformation + implicit CTA.
- **URL slug:** `/case-studies/[customer-slug]` or `/customer-stories/[customer-name]-[industry]-case-study`
- **Open Graph / Twitter Card:** mirror title/description; use `summary_large_image` card type.
- **Heading hierarchy:** single H1 (the headline); H2 for major framework sections; H3 for sub-points. Never skip levels.
- **Internal links:** to relevant feature pages, other case studies, and pricing — using descriptive anchor text, not "click here."
- **Schema:** `Article`/`WebPage` + `Organization`; add `FAQ` schema if an FAQ section is present.
- **Keywords:** primary ("UniqBrio [industry] case study"), secondary ("academy management software," "sports academy management system India"), semantic ("attendance automation," "fee collection software," "enrollment growth"). Place naturally in H1, a few H2s, meta description, URL, and image alt text — never keyword-stuff.
- **Image alt text:** descriptive and specific (e.g. "Graph showing a reported 35% increase in on-time fee collection for [Academy]").

## 13. Conversion Optimization

- **CTA placement:** hero, after the Challenge section (mid-page), and a strong final CTA — 3–5 touchpoints total, never more than feels natural.
- **Proof hierarchy on the page:** Customer identity → Metrics → Evidence/attribution → Implementation detail → Quote → CTA.
- **Trust signals:** named customer where permitted, deployment scope, years in business, verified metrics.
- **Risk reduction:** reference free trial, low-friction onboarding, and support availability where true.
- **Objection handling:** proactively address common doubts (implementation time, ease of use for non-technical staff, data security, cost) either in-line or via the FAQ section.
- **Decision-stage messaging:** direct, specific language at the close ("Book a personalized demo to see how UniqBrio can simplify fee collection for your academy") rather than generic ("Contact us").

## 14. Accessibility

- Single H1, strictly logical heading hierarchy (no skipped levels).
- Plain language; avoid jargon and idiom.
- Reading level Grade 8–10.
- Never convey information by color alone — pair color cues with text/icons.
- Properly structured tables with header cells for any before/after comparison data.
- Captions for any video/audio content.
- Descriptive alt text for every image (see §12).
- Descriptive link text ("Download the full case study PDF," not "click here").
- Statistics presented in a format readable by screen readers (not solely inside an image or chart).

## 16. Checklists

**Pre-writing:** customer identified and approved to participate · interview conducted · notes transcribed · quantified metrics identified where available · supporting evidence gathered · working outline drafted.

**Research:** review available analytics/CRM/support logs · review industry and regional context · review customer's public presence (website, social).

**Interview:** structured questionnaire prepared · chronological pain points captured · "why UniqBrio" decision rationale explored · quantified results captured · verbatim quotable lines captured · future plans discussed.

**Draft:** framework followed (§4) · narrative flows Challenge→Solution→Result · every metric qualified appropriately (§6) · quotes marked pending approval · CTA present at 3+ points.

**Review:** accuracy checked line-by-line · tone/consistency checked · no hype/buzzwords remain · narrative flow confirmed.

**SEO:** title/meta/slug set · heading hierarchy correct · keywords natural, not stuffed · schema recommended · alt text drafted.

**Legal:** written customer approval obtained · no confidential terms disclosed without clearance · no defamatory or unverifiable claims.

**Fact verification:** every name/title/date correct · every metric re-checked against its source · every quote re-checked against the transcript.

**Accessibility:** heading order logical · alt text present · no color-only signaling · tables properly structured · descriptive link text.

**Publishing:** final draft approved · visuals in place or clearly placeholdered · metadata live · CTAs tested · page live at correct URL.

**Maintenance / Quarterly review:** metrics still accurate and current · links still functional · customer's situation still accurately represented · consider adding newer results if available.

## 17. Quality Standards Rubric

Pass/fail against each criterion before publishing:

| Criterion | Pass condition |
|---|---|
| Accuracy | No unsupported factual claims; all facts verified |
| Specificity | Concrete academy-specific detail, not generic SaaS language |
| Evidence | Every major claim has an identifiable, attributed source |
| Quantification | At least 3 distinct, appropriately-qualified metrics (or explicit qualitative justification if none exist) |
| Narrative flow | Clear chronological Challenge→Solution→Result progression |
| Readability | Grade 8–10 reading level, short paragraphs, scannable formatting |
| SEO | Metadata, heading hierarchy, and natural keyword placement complete |
| Conversion | CTAs present, relevant, and well-placed |
| Accessibility | Passes §14 checklist |
| Trustworthiness | No hype, no fabricated statistics or quotes |
| Completeness | All framework sections present or transparently marked as unavailable |

## 18. Edge Cases

- **No metrics available:** pivot to qualitative transformation narrative (time saved, stress reduced, morale improved); use honest qualifiers; never manufacture numbers.
- **Anonymous customers:** use a descriptive stand-in ("A leading Tier-2 martial arts academy in Gujarat"); no invented names or logos.
- **Incomplete interviews:** fill gaps with internal usage data where available and clearly attributed; state plainly what is unknown rather than guessing.
- **Enterprise customers:** expand on governance, security, stakeholder involvement, and formal rollout; expect a more rigorous legal review.
- **Very small customers:** emphasize ease of use, speed to value, and owner-level impact rather than enterprise-scale metrics.
- **Early success / early-stage results:** clearly frame as "initial results" within a stated timeframe; do not present early wins as mature, stable outcomes.
- **Long implementation:** document phases honestly; frame as a deliberate, supported journey rather than glossing over delays.
- **Failed-implementation lessons:** if the customer is willing to share, frame around adjustment and recovery — challenge, adjustment, learning, outcome — without assigning blame.
- **Pilot programs:** clearly separate pilot-phase results from full-deployment results.
- **Multi-location academies:** explain rollout sequencing by branch; highlight centralized/standardized outcomes.
- **Seasonal businesses:** compare equivalent seasonal periods; avoid misleading off-season/peak-season comparisons.
- **Missing screenshots:** use a clearly labeled design placeholder; never describe a visual that doesn't exist.
- **Sensitive industries/information:** reduce identifying detail and redact confidential figures as needed.

## 19. Examples

**Headlines**
- Weak: "How XYZ Academy Uses UniqBrio to Manage Students."
- Strong: "How XYZ Martial Arts Academy Increased Fee Collection by 40% and Saved 15 Hours a Week with UniqBrio."

**Quantified outcomes**
- Weak: "UniqBrio helped them save a lot of time on admin work."
- Strong: "By automating WhatsApp payment reminders, the admin team reduced manual fee-chasing from roughly 12 hours a week to about 2."

**Quotes**
- Poor: "UniqBrio is a revolutionary game-changer for our academy. The synergy is amazing."
- Effective: "Before UniqBrio, I spent my Sundays calling parents about pending fees. Now the automated WhatsApp reminders collect most dues before I even open my laptop." — [Name], Founder, [Academy]

**CTAs**
- Weak: "Click here to learn more."
- Strong: "See how UniqBrio can automate your fee collection — book a 15-minute demo."

**Narratives**
- Weak: "Problem. Software. Success."
- Strong: "As enrollment grew across multiple instructors, administrative complexity grew with it. Manual attendance registers and fragmented payment tracking created daily friction. After evaluating a few alternatives, the academy rolled out UniqBrio in phases — starting with attendance and fees before expanding into scheduling and reporting — and staff gradually adopted standardized workflows, leading to more consistent operations according to customer-reported outcomes."
